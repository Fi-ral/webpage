Preamble

This guide is specifically designed to be used in conjunction with the Standards Technology list and is not standalone. While it will not apply to every setting due to the technological differences, some concepts are still useful when designing your own vessels.

It will also not go much into the specifics about applying numbers to specific components, as this is something I personally find very distasteful; rather, it goes into the general decisions one might want to make to create something that looks and feels realistic rather than be realistic. Internal consistency is far more important than rigorous science to this end. While this website does offer a variety of calculators that can be used to that end, they are not designed for that and are mostly intended as an estimation tool. After all, if I wanted to compare spreadsheets, I would do that at work and not when reading science fiction stories.

Military Guide

Getting started

The most important thing when designing a vessel is determining what it is supposed to be doing in the first place and how much you can afford to spend on it. Not every ship needs to or even can do everything and the clearer your choices are here, the easier it is to come up with a good design with a clearly defined role it needs to fill.

Some important ones, in no particular order are:

  • Is it supposed to fight pirates or is it supposed to fight against the Empire?
  • Is it supposed to be mass produced, one line of production or is it a unique design?
  • Are there already similar vessels in service and if so, what do they look like?
  • What technology is available for military use for the faction building them?
  • What vessels does your most likely enemy field and how do you counter them?
  • Are you the dominant player, in a defensive role or is the playing field more even?
  • How do those choices compare to the choices of whoever you are fighting?

Tradeoffs

Once you have some basic ideas about the design, it is time to talk about the tradeoffs you will need to take. For any given mass and power budget, there are only so many things you can stick onto a spaceship, after all, and still keep the engine going and the lights on. While not exhaustive, it might be useful to think of this as a triangle between how many weapons, how many defenses and how much engine you can stick onto it before you overrun on either of the two constricting factors.

Graph originally by Winchell Chung on Project Rho


The only way you can put more onto a vessel would then, consequently be, to increase your overall budget which also has its natural limits. For one, you now need to increase both the engine if you want the same performance as before, and you need to armor a larger surface area against threats, which can become very expensive if you rely on physical armor rather than just an active defense grid. Where exactly you fall will obviously depend on the threats you face. If pirates are your primary concern, you might not need as much defenses as they will lack heavy weaponry, allowing you to invest more into propulsion or weapons. Conversely, if you expect the ships to be under heavy fire, it might be worth undergunning them a bit to get more defense in so they can be in the fight for longer and do more overall work.

Some other factors might also include how well it can integrate with the rest of your fleet. If your superspacegun can kill anything but it requires an entire new logistical chain to keep operating, it might not be worth it despite being super awesome. Strategic endurance, sensor capability, and command ability are also factors one might consider when designing a vessel but will tend to have lower effects on the aforementioned constraints.

Overall though, the tradeoffs you take should align the role of the craft and the larger doctrine it is operating within to ensure it can meet its requirements even if that comes at the cost of being worse at some specific things you might want it to be able to do.

"Do-it-all" Ships

Anyone who knows me, knows that I dislike ships designed to fulfill every role imaginable and hate battlecarriers. This is why. Any choice is eliminated, making "do-it-all" ships usually a bad idea from a multitude of perspectives, while offering little advantages over multiple designs.

In-universe, they will end up being much more expensive than they need to be, allowing you to field far fewer of them than you’d like to have. Alternatively, if they are constructed cheaper than would be required, they end up being worse at at least one task, or, if you are unfortunate enough, all of them, making the entire design worthless. Out of -universe, it collapses almost all tactics you can give the reader. Formations stop mattering as every vessel is either identical or, if there are multiple generations of vessels, strictly worse at all tasks it could be given. All battles become a giant death ball without much rhyme or reason and little ability for individual characters to exert outsized influence.

Battlecarriers, ships that act as both a heavy-weapons platform (i.e. a battleship) and a launch vehicle (i.e. a carrier), are a particularly egregious offender. Capital ships should almost never try and spec into too many things. They are designed to kill other enemy capital ships and nothing else. If that ends up requiring big guns, you give them big guns; if it ends up requiring fighters, you give them fighters. If either works, you build two different designs to work in conjunction, but you do not combine them.

That all said, there are valid reasons to want multirole vessels. For example, if they will face unpredictable mission profiles, operate far away from home, and need that extra flexibility, or if a smaller force cannot afford to build dedicated vessels for every task. But even in those situations, they are not something most admirals would want in their fleet unless they had to put them there because no other options were available.

The existence of such vessels can be fine depending on what you are trying to write. If you are not interested in the overall state of a battle because your POV characters are running off doing something else, then it does not really matter how things are progressing exactly, but that also probably means you do not need to describe individual ships either to begin with.

Doctrines

It should be noted that the exact doctrines used by any given state-level actor will depend on their exact situation they find themselves in, both in absolute and relative terms, and on the technology available, which is why it is not possible to generalize any tactics and strategies and declare them universally as the best (least of all for me). For the purposes of the remainder of this article, though, it is assumed that the actor is in a roughly peer-to-near-peer-level situation with technology from the standards technology list. For the sake of completeness, I will nevertheless briefly touch on two other, potentially not too uncommon scenarios first. These being anti-pirate and/or low-level conflicts, and asymmetric technological distribution in their respective chapters.

Conventional Wars

Conventional wars consist of two parties that are (roughly) evenly matched fighting each other's military directly. The forces on each side are well-defined, and the conflict is deliberately not fought with weapons of mass destruction. This, for the purposes of creating doctrines around, is the ideal situation. You know exactly what kind of enemy you are fighting, what they are capable of, what tactics and strategies they are likely to use, and, importantly, what they will not do.

These wars can, however, still range widely in scope from a localized border dispute all the way to a total war. As such, the surrounding doctrine can also vary massively, though some core tenets will stay identical: The end goal is not the total destruction of the enemy but the achievement of whatever pre-war goals and objectives have been laid out and that are the duty for the military to execute. While this can certainly include the total elimination of enemy forces as was seen in the Second World War, this is typically not required and too expensive to consider. Keeping the scope as small as possible for your end goals is your friend, whether you are the attacker or defender; wars are expensive. If you want to conquer somebody’s far-flung colony, you do not need to conquer their capital to force them to capitulate. Instead, it is enough to wipe out the local garrison and make reinforcement too costly or outright impossible.

Now how do you go about this? Again, this heavily depends on the exact situation you find yourself in, but, as before, there are some core tenets that will not fail you:

  • Concentration: Concentrate your combat power at the decisive place and time and do not waste resources where they are not needed. You do not need to have superiority of force everywhere but only where you want to strike to dislodge your adversary.
  • Maneuver: Place the enemy in a disadvantageous position through flexible movement. If you can reposition faster than the enemy, be that strategically or tactically, you can force them into situations they would rather not be in while they cannot do the same to you.
  • Initiative: If you have the initiative, you have power to dictate the time, place, and terms of action in a conflict, compelling the opponent to respond defensively which further limits the range of response they can give you in return

In short, be there where your enemy isn't, win fast and limit your victory to what you actually want instead of pushing your luck.

Low-Level Wars

A low-level conflict is defined as "an armed struggle between states or groups that stays below the level of a full-scale conventional war" which, by its very nature, limits the resources available to fight other potential peer-adversaries, either due to political, moral, or simply logistical issues, warranting a change in approach. While not always the case, such conflict is most likely to arise in situations with an asymmetric distribution of power between the two combating parties, which will be the basis of this idea, though it may also happen between two otherwise even-leveled forces if greater powers are preventing a full escalation. Other potential scenarios could include counter-terror, anti-piracy, or anti-militia operations. In any case and unlike what was discussed prior, it is either not possible or unwanted to deploy full force against them.

Due to this, it is also likely not possible to force frontline combat or a decisive battle, with the goal, much like with conventional war, not being the total (or even partial) destruction of the enemy through combat rendering their continued operation impossible, but instead to render said continued opposition too costly to continue under current conditions, be that financially or politically. This goes for both the militarily dominant side and the militarily inferior side. It is therefore the most logical move to decrease one's own capital investment as much as possible while, inversely, increasing the cost for the other side to continue. This makes the deployment of large capital vessels ill-advised outside of support roles far away from combat, instead shifting most of the burden onto the smaller escort craft that are more flexible and cheaper to operate. The advantage of this is twofold; they are, as mentioned, cheaper simply by virtue of being smaller, and a loss is not as destructive in terms of materiel and/or personnel cost.

While technically possible with any such escort unit, and this will often be the case if an actor has other military obligations, it might be more cost-effective to use dedicated craft or ones retrofitted for this purpose. Such modifications can include, but are not limited to:

  • the reduction or elimination of peer-level weapons
  • the addition of cheaper but less effective weapons
  • improvements on operational endurance

These might seem similar to what we discussed already, but in this case there is the unique advantage of being able to more precisely anticipate what kind of weapons will be needed to be defended against. For instance, it is unlikely for a militia or pirate force to possess dedicated anti-ship weapons, or for a state that does possesses them to escalate unprovoked and risk retaliation unprompted, making more aggressive skimming on defensive capability possible.

As a final note, it is worth noting that this all relies on the fact that both sides are able to engage in this sort of warfare, which, unfortunately, has not been the case historically, often leading to the erasure of civilian populations to prevent insurgencies from forming.
